The Future of Software: A Car, Not a Teleportation Machine

A reflection on AI tools, software economics, and why human ingenuity still matters.

The Economics of Building Software Today

Something new I learnt recently - are the profit margins of the AI companies now-a-days. It was contrary to my belief that software is always scalable and is the best business to be in. [link 1 Link 2] If you're thinking about building AI apps, consider this: AI app margins hover around 20–30%. A well-built B2B SaaS product? 80% (or even 90%). The math is clear. Instead of chasing AI-first products, build genuinely great SaaS.. and enhance it with AI where it makes sense.

A truly valuable piece of software should be valuable even without AI. If you strip the AI layer away and your product collapses, you don't have a product.. you have a wrapper. This isn't a stance against AI. It's just economics. If you want to operate in the 80%+ margin bracket, your core product has to stand on its own.

What I've Actually Seen

I've been deep in the AI tooling world. I've worked with Llama, Qwen, Mistral, and the smaller models. I've used Cursor, Claude, Codex. And yesterday evening, something clicked.

Yes, these tools generate code fast. Impressively fast. But wiring that output into something production-ready is still a real challenge. When I compare this to how I used to build applications before 2022, the old way was more reliable in a specific sense.. every small module was tested, understood, and you knew exactly how everything flowed.

Now, things are quicker. But handling complexity is the bottleneck. LLMs can generate hundreds of files at once. How much of that do you actually understand? Would you be able to debug it end-to-end if something broke at 2 AM?

Two Ways to Build with AI

There are essentially two approaches right now.

The full sprint: One-shot generation. Point the LLM at your spec and let it generate everything at once.. dozens of files, hundreds of lines. It's fast. But when something breaks (and it will), you're stuck reverse-engineering code you didn't write.

The measured approach: Define your framework first. Build file skeletons. Generate each file with guardrails. Review as you go. This is significantly slower than the full sprint. But you understand every phase. You know what each file does, why it exists, and how it connects to the rest of the system.

The second approach is still faster than writing everything by hand, debugging syntax errors, and searching Stack Overflow. But it respects an important principle: knowing how your software is built matters.

Speed is valuable. But understanding is essential. If you don't know the basics.. if you've never written a loop, never debugged a memory leak, never designed an API.. how will you know when the AI gets it wrong? And more importantly, how will you invent something new?

** If you are new to coding in the era of AI, yes - you need to do the grind and build the muscle memory to construct systems and know the principles behind it - to the point where you can write code and develop systems without AI. Having said that, AI can be used as a tutor in this phase (It is an extremely patient tutor and an incredible accelerator for learning, I talk to it anytime i need to learn something new and want to research more), but not something that does your job.

A Car Over a Horse Carriage

This is where we are. What we have right now is a car.. not a teleporting machine.

Teleportation has been a vision since science fiction existed. And yet, for personal everyday transport, we're still at cars. That's exactly where AI tooling sits today. We've built something that emulates human thought remarkably well. But there are edge cases where it cannot match human ingenuity.. the way we hold complexity in our heads, the intuition we bring to system design, the understanding of why something should be built a certain way.

Why This Makes Me Happy

I'm not delighted because AI isn't good enough. It's extremely good. I'm delighted because I finally understand what it actually is.

We have a tool that lets us do what we used to do.. but faster. That's powerful. But the talent, the understanding of how systems work, the deep coding knowledge.. all of that is still essential. If you never learn the basics, if you never understand how things came to be, how will you ever invent?

A Note to Myself

I'm very confident that AI is a tool.. a remarkable one.. and not an existential threat to software careers. Instead of being scared, I'm excited. We've invented something that is a great assistant.

Does this app solve a real problem without AI?

If the answer is no.. relax. That's not replacing your job.

Optimism makes me happy. Maybe it's just a choice. But it's my choice, and I'm happy with it.

To whoever is reading this.. good luck, and don't worry, we are here, and will be here.
